# The continuous ranked probability score (CRPS)

The continuous ranked probability score (CRPS) is a scoring function
that compares forecast ensembles to observations based on the comparison
of their cumulative distribution functions. It is a useful score to
measure accuracy and sharpness in predicted ensembles. Stochastic models
and Bayesian methods deal with distributions, hence the CRPS enables
quantitative comparison of forecasts and can be used as a loss function
to train models in machine learning.

In what follows, we let $x_i, ~i=1,\ldots, M$ denote our forecast and
let $y$ denote the observation. The ensemble has an empirical cumulative
distribution function (cdf) $F$, defined as

$$F(s) = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^M\mathbb{I}_{\{s\geq x_i\}}.$$ 

Based on the CDF, the CRPS is defined as

$$\mathrm{CRPS}(F, y) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty\! \left(F(s) - \mathbb{I}_{\{s\geq y\}}\right)^2\,\text{d}s.$$


The above definition allows us to compare a forecast distribution with a
single observation. This is cleverly done by using the Heaviside step
function $\mathbb{I}$, which serves as the empirical CDF of the
observation. We see that the CRPS is zero if the forecast distribution
is a Dirac delta at the observation $y$, i.e., if the forecast perfectly
matches the observation. This may not always be possible; some
discrepancy with the observation is expected. In that case, the squared
term ensures that outliers are penalized strongly, hence both accuracy
and sharpness lead to a low CRPS value. Furthermore, the CRPS is a
*strictly proper scoring rule*, meaning that the lowest value is
attained when the forecast ensemble exactly coincides with the
observation.

For our discrete forecast ensemble, the CRPS can be approximated by

$$\mathrm{CRPS}(x, y) = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^M \left| x_i - y\right| - \frac{1}{2M^2}\sum_{i=1}^M \sum_{j=1}^M \left| x_i - x_j\right|.$$

The first term measures the average absolute error between the forecast
and the observation (accuracy), while the second term penalizes spread
within the ensemble (sharpness). This formula shows how the CRPS can be
considered a probabilistic extension of the mean absolute error (MAE):
when all $x_i$ have the same value (that is, when the distribution
collapses to a single point), then the above equation reduces to the MAE
[[1]](#1).

### Relation to other scoring rules

The CRPS is related to other skill scores.

The *Brier score* is a proper scoring rule for binary events. It
essentially measures the mean squared error for events that can be
categorized as 'true' or 'false', and can be used when classifying model
results. The CRPS reduces to the Brier score in the case of binary
outcomes.

A multivariate generalization of the CRPS is the *energy score*
[[2]](#2), which defines a strictly proper scoring rule
for vector-valued ensembles. The sample energy score is computed
analogously to the sample CRPS, replacing the absolute values by vector
norms.

In the scalar case, the CRPS is an extension of the *Wasserstein-1
distance* that penalizes ensemble spread. Namely, the sample Wasserstein
distance between the forecast ensemble and the delta distribution of the
observation is the first term appearing in the sample CRPS. The addition
of the dispersion penalty in the sample CRPS makes it a strictly proper
scoring rule.
